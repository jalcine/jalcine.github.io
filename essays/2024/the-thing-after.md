I’ve been fighting with myself on what the “next generation” of my online presence would look like - and if I needed to
have one. Ever since [I’ve decided to align myself][1] with the tenants of the IndieWeb, around using personal Websites
as our canonical presence online, I’ve begun a nosedive into what that really meant. Ideologically, it has a
left-leaning libertarian tilt as it focuses on individual development as a growth marker for community development - as
opposed to a community of people building things that are collectively used[^1], something more in the ethos of the
documented hacktivist nature of the 90s. As someone who holds the position that [technology is political][2], this idea
of a self-expression in a space that was already defining everything through echos of corporately-sponsored online
presences _really_ rang with me. But the focus on “build for you first, then for others” runs so counter to my
understanding of the world both from a cultural and political standpoint. That didn’t stop me from even wanting to talk
more about it in community spaces to my friends _outside_ of tech and in any space I could (even in organizing).  I kept
musing on the Website as a digital “twin” of myself (if not my personally authorized form of digital self-expression).
Not only was the composition of the site brought into question, but organic discovery remains in my head as well as a
suite of other questions:

* _How do I find you again_? Was it by one’s uniform resource locator or URL (most people call these links, if even
  that) or do I use your number/email/username-you-commonly-use?
* _Where do I use this locator_ (in a Web browser’s address bar/“the Internet app” or do I have to use another
  application)?
* How do I let someone see this but only give _them_ the permission to do so (magic link/password/single-sign-on
  IndieAuth)?
* _Could I convince or show this to someone else_ not as aligned to this perspective on online representation and have
  them engage with it (spacehey versus cohost, Mastodon/AP versus BlueSky/AT)?
* What can _resistance look like_ if we don’t know how it works, let alone _exactly_ what data it’s using, what it folds
  in relation and how to ascertain _why_ this was aligned to you with no manipulation? There’s less of a need for one to
  be overtly _political_ to be pushed into the darkest paths of non-discovery (algorithms that aim to provide a
  suggestion by aggregate with trends, popularity and endless streams), as this is now “implied” by the kinds of people
  you’d engage - if even that. **Most platforms don’t disclose how content come across our feeds**.

It’s dangerous to discount the role that profit has in shaping how we perceive things. Something as simple
as being able to see a preview of the contents of a link, when put against near-infinite feeds, can slide into
encouragement of tighter argument loops when the user experience of online conversation in conventional social media
moves towards argumentation (though this is dismissive of how society and our bias also influences our behaviors and
decisions online - especially of the Reply Guy). Such a behavior _is_ tracked (and can be indirectly determined by
trends, view counts and parsing what one _might_ be saying from analysis) and _used_ to shape what _it_ thinks you want
to see. It's fair to say that I'm projecting a lot of my ideas of the Web onto the IndieWeb. I think I took the phrase
"people-focused" in the byline way too seriously. That shouldn't be terribly surprising [given how I approach
technology][6] as a whole.

I’d like to report saying that I see (yet!) another but familiar idea on crafting online presences that reflect us in
the highest fidelity possible to help build communities. Sadly, I’m _way_ too pessimistic about how such an ecosystem
could be built and abused by bad actors (the State, corporations, right-wing ideologues and the very sporadic “lone
black-hat hacker” all come to mind) and each time I get closer to this, I put it down and find something else to do (or
let go from work, wrapped up with life, tied up with organizing - this is always at the bottom of my priority list). It
doesn’t help that after reading Klein’s [Doppelganger][3], my thoughts on this have evolved even more. What does it mean
for the future perspectives of the social Web if the majority of it replicate what we see as the vices of capitalist
organizations that organize our *social engagements* in favor of being engaged with their platform over anything else? I
don’t think we take enough time to imagine a new environment away from this and focus on a need to be first, to “tinker
in the open” without acknowledging that the “open” is occupied by many other people and one loose screw equates to a
DDoS of a mutual aid campaign into oblivion. I think these both come partly with the landscape of tech - it has
carry-over from the hyper-competitive nature of corporate business wrapped in the language of meritocracy, privileged
(and yet limited) creativity and an obsession with capital (social, fiscal) at any cost.

On the other hand, the flexibility and power of the Web that we have today compared to that of 2003 is something that's
to be lauded. I didn’t start my journey into the Web for some time after that, but even then I remember the extensive
use of area maps and tables to provide graphically rich experiences. Now we have flexbox, `rem`s and variables in CSS!
You can stop doing the (weird but clever!) thing with anchor links in the URL to store path history! SVGs! WebSockets!
We have a lot and returning the Web back to people in a way that also gives people these things in their hands is
something we owe people when we try to re-imagine what kind of platforms or tools we want people to use to make the Web
with. WordPress has been working on this with [their Blocks project][blocks] and more companies like Webflow are
allowing people to eschew using direct code to make experiences on the Web. But what does the community have to provide
as a tangential answer, in order to prevent another entrapment of private companies being able to define how we convene?

As I’ve opened, I’ve contributed some thoughts and ideas to the IndieWeb. From my [user page on the collective wiki of
the IndieWeb community][8], you can see what places I touched - from small typographical changes to whole pages about
projects and ideas to flesh out. I’m not really sure _what_ that amounted to. I _do_ see perceive some of the ideas of
the IndieWeb to be quite radical: a want to demonstrate a personalized expression of self in a landscape where the
de-facto forms of representation are sterilized, homogenized and about what new blogging platform ones moved their site
to (which I do enjoy reading, everyone does something a _little_ different). We've also seen a lot of folks do zany
things with the web, we got calls to [re-weird the Web][4] or a [sort of eulogy to what "could have been"][5]. And on
the far edges, we see people providing tools and ideas on how to break away from a need to rely on companies to provide
always-on connectivity, something that's become more important to me for many reasons (fiscal, environmental and the
like).

But **making a website isn't enough anymore**; it perhaps never was. More directly, it's not enough to spend money on a
domain name and rent some space on - which are requirements to get the maximum benefits of digital autonomy, a
requirement for consent. Folks use places like Pinterest to collect things that they find interesting, Reddit to have
discussions on things that .. well, provoke them to type and Twitter is where Chris Messina reminds us constantly that
he invented the hashtag. Each of these places come with communities, that were built over time, and declare them in the
forefront. Beyond the personal website, a more salient answer for managing our communities - or just _defining_ them is
going to be necessary. This information is currently captured in what's sometimes called one's social graph[^2]. Younger
generations have less of a concern of holding onto these and lean on what they already have and know - names, numbers
and maybe e-mail - to find each other on other places; showing DNS, URLs and the like as a concept to be something for
"nerds and power-users". That's a big reason why that TikTok, Instagram and other places deploy so many UX barriers to
just search for folks outside of those factors on your mobile device. We've "lost" the fight to demonstrate the value of
keeping such information in part of a lack of reliable, non-corporatized ways to do so. No one _owns_ a username, unless
you have the weight of the USPTO or an equivalent in your jurisdiction. And even then, nothing stops a parody account
from [presenting as the such][7]. And that's _excellent_ as it allows people to define who's authoritative to each other
(with the obvious downside that a bad actor like anyone wishing to spread propaganda masquerading as a trusted source
could get past things, even if done as a "joke").
