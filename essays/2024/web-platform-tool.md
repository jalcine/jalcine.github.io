The open social Web is rife with thoughts, opinions - _lots of opinions_ - and work from its advocates to push folks into a
space where digital feudalism isn't the end-all-be-all of our online engagements. In reading [Walter Rodney][wr]'s [How Europe
Underdeveloped Africa][eua], he notes how communalism in Africa led to a bit of a time _buffer_ to feudalism in comparsion to its
European counterparts:

> In feudal societies, there were clashes between the landlord and peasent masses and later on between the landlord and
> merchant classes. Under capitalism, the principal class contradition inside Europe was between the proletariat and the
> bourgeoisiee. Those hostile class relations provided the motive force within the respective societies. African
> communal socities had differences such as age-grades and differences between ordianry members and religious leaders
> such as rainmakers. However, those were not expliotative or antagonistic relations. The concept of class as a motive
> force in social development had not yet come about; and in the communal phase, one must look at the fundamental forces
> of production to understand the process of change.

Communalism is a practice that has loose hierachy but high quality communication. With enough care and design, online
platforms can be built in such a way that both reduces bus factors and keeps people safe. My opinion is that in order to
do this, we need to work on to avoid walking back to feudal-esque systems and consider more "village" or "town" infrastructures.
It might seem a bit _odd_ to reference this kind of material for defining and designing digital spaces. It should also
be equally as odd that the descendants of people in these spaces have been pushed to be a bit of an afternote or a means
of preventing ridicule later down the road[^4]; these practices can help guide a safer community (and even help us model
better ones offline).

## Town Infrastructure
The coinage isn't novel nor is the practice but the idea is sound. One of having people collectively (on an opt-in basis) be
both stewards (if not shippers) of the communities we build and the members of authority that can have a direct vote in
how their environments are shaped. Companies like BlueSky and [their protocol][1] managed to incorporate parts of this into their
platform[^1] and in turn, built a stronger community of safety. There's ventures like [IFTAS][2] that provide the
backing of a trust and safety department you'd find in Dorseyian Twitter[^2] and produces accessible
[documentation about state policy changes][3] that affect our ability to connect. The implementations of this, though,
are locked by those governing these platforms. BlueSky's final decision maker lies in the hands of one person[^3]
and Mastodon to [Eugen][4]. It relies on external pressure to mobilize these people (or their delegated parties) to
deploy change that community sees fit.

The conversation on how to design politics for digital spaces is blossoming[^5]. What's new is our ability to both design
spaces _and_ bridge them to the ones that exist today at speeds _far faster than expected_. Instead of waiting for API
access to be approved, contracts to be settled or the like, we have put forth the notion that information can be both
