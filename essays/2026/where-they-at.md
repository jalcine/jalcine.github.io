A point that might be one of grievance [of a lost space never truly owned by
Black people but still run by it][1] tends to pop up on my "Following" feed on
Bluesky once a month. I don't see such conversations on places like Threads
&mdash; it's really a insular space of "you see what the algorithm determines
worthy of attention". It comes from a variation of folks but routinely from
folks who seem to long for a space that no longer exist. Yes - Twitter, now X -
the company still exists, but there's a heavily undocumented[^1] shift in the
entity that was once Black Twitter and how the next option for many was the
once-dubbed [P92 project][p92], known as Threads to many today. A friend and expert,
[Dr. Meredith Clark][15], notes when speaking to [the University of
Virginia][3], defined Black Twitter as:

> a network of _culturally connected communicators_ using the platform to draw
> attention to issues of concern to [B]lack communities. **It’s the culture that we
> grew up with**. It’s the culture that we experienced in our lives and school,
> in the workplace, with entertainment – and you see conversations coalesce
> around specific cultural moments. I always explain to people that Black
> Twitter doesn’t have a gateway, a secret knock. It’s not a separate platform.
> It’s all in the way that people use the platform to draw attention to issues
> of concern to black communities.

This is why, despite using the ethnic marker most commonly used for African
Americans, it's able to hold folks from the Caribbean, South America and Europe
&mdash; although due to the name of the label, African American culture (and
those geographically closer to it) are more represented in it. In a beautiful
way, Black Twitter was a way for young and old African Americans to
communicate with one another in real time _in public_ without explicit
oversight or control by school boards, the FCC, Hollywood and what have you.
Ironically, events on Black Twitter have managed to transcend the space from
which they began themselves - like [the Temecula incident][4] or the indie film
that introduced [Taylour Paige][6] to use, [Zola][5]. This is backed by the
research done by [Pew that showed Black users][7] to be _more active_ on these
platforms than that other demographics.

I can continue to repeat the academic research that's been put into
understanding Twitter as a concept when it came to Black people and I'll list
some here:

- [Dr. Andre Brock's][8]: ["From the Blackhand Side: Twitter as a Cultural
  Conversation"][8]
- ["Distributed Blackness"][9] by the same author, formative in shaping
  [Blacksky][10]
- [Laur M. Jackson][11] on [The Blackness of Meme Movement][12]
- [Evette Dionne][13] on [In Memoriam of Black Twitter][14]
- [Dr. Meredith Clark][15] on ["We Tried To Tell Y'all"][16] and her seminal essay,
  [To Tweet Our Own Cause: A Mixed-Methods Study of the Online Phenomenon "Black
  Twitter"][17]

I'm not expecting _everyone_ to know of all of the works above &mdash; I work
on [social media technologies][18] and have been in rooms _on behalf of_
sections of Black Twitter &mdash; a burden and privilege that carries a weight
in how the technologies we use to connect and relate are shaped. But by
engaging in these institutions with these titles - it reinforces become a
problem of phrasing and context when folks choose to bemoan the presence of
Black people on the platform.

## 'It's The Truth: There's More Black People At...'

To use a computational metaphor &mdash; if there's 1,000 people in room A and
100 of them are Black and in room B, there's 200 people and 10 of them are also
Black; by numbers &mdash; there is more Black people in room A then they are in
room B. Using statistical language, this is a "raw count of absolute
frequencies". It's also very apparent in a homogenized ecosystem. Bluesky PBLLC
took a different approach in their invention of [the AT Protocol][19] that gave
way to [Blacksky][10]. Despite [running in a way that can make the word
"distributed" in Brock's book more computationally sound][21], Bluesky _dwarfs_
the number of users that Blacksky has on a rate of nearly 7,000 to 1. Blacksky
manages to deter this by implementing a feed that prioritizes folks who
explicitly opt-in to being in Blacksky, the flagship feed of
<https://blacksky.community/>, by using [#AddToBlacksky][20]. There's about
14,300 people who liked this feed (a soft indicator of use). These numbers are
going to seem small to accounts who have _thousands of followers_ in the Bluesky
ecosystem, a feat given that it reports about a million active users daily of
their ~44 million users.

This equivalency is needed because it shows that despite the four-odd years in
Bluesky's (and less in Blacksky's case) existence, they've managed to develop
an _organic_ audience without advertising or algorithmic engagement boosting
&mdash; things that Facebook and Twitter have both used in ways to increase
their profits (with limited revenue sharing to the creators who power their
platform and the journalists who live on there).

So _yes_, there's more Black people on the mega-platform owned by Facebook
that's 20 years old than there is on Bluesky - in a similar way that [Brazil holds
more Black people than the United States by a ratio of nearly 3 to 1][22]. That
statement by itself will seem confusing, given that African Americans are more
likely to have a higher quality of life (in terms of avoidance of racial
oppression, political representation and mobility, access to education and what
have you) but that occurs when _historical, economic and cultural_ context is
stripped from a perspective.

## Going Where the People Are

As with the difficulty of describing all of those of African ancestry in the
United States (partially or otherwise) under a singular umbrella term, the want
to describe (enforce?) a (non-)consensual understanding of Blackness on a
sub-section of the Internet as this singular entity, one as neatly described as
"Black Twitter", has been a desire of folks whose role in the media production
functions of society have left them currently without a faucet to tap on when
they want to know "what's going on" ever since Twitter's sale to Elon. Threads
has benefited from a large density of Black people on the platform
automatically having accounts by way of Instagram[^3] - a digital equivalent to
the benefit that the United States had on its cultural outputs due to its
capture of African people from centuries ago and their persistent grasp on
preventing a means of legitimate exit (if not disassociation).

It's interesting, though. As much as it's spoken about &mdash; we don't see
other places having issues like this. Smaller ones, in comparison to places
like Threads or Blacksky, don't seem to have this problem. It could be due to
the fact that they didn't branch off from other spaces. The most prominent one
that I pay attention to is Spill. They managed to secure funds in venture
capital of [two million from Kerry Washington][23] in 2024. Ironically, they're
part of the inspiration of my idea of [Spadat][24] for AT Protocol. But they're not
honest about how they make money _literally_ off their analysis of the slice of
digital Black experiences &mdash; not to their users, nor do these people who
pay the executive times get paid out for this, [being able to pay back Kerry
$0.50 to the dollar][25] of her investment (if they paid it out quick):

> According to Spill’s chief growth officer, Kenya Parham, the company
> projected it would cross **$1 million in annualized revenue** at the start of
> 2025 and had surpassed that mark by **the end of the third quarter of 2025**.

I bring this up mainly because there's a similar objective from folks who "want
to be where the Black people are" but ignore _any_ formation of the such (or
use the topic of it as material on other platforms). It's not really about
building communities but **more about looking for ways to juice engagement back
into things that they own and run**. We've seen it with Buzzfeed and Worldstar
and now Facebook (and Tiktok) has brought the bar down so low for this type of
engagement farming, there's a genre of folks who feel _entitled_ to it.

For example, Spill doesn't work with local organizations when they run their
conversations about sexual health. Instead, they feed this AI-driven analysis
(as paid market research) to large pharmaceutical companies (who can then
tailor those ads you see _outside_ of their platform to be more "culturally
relevant" or make marketing material more targeted):

> Spill’s approach matters only if marketers can translate it into campaigns
> that meet their objectives. One of the clearest examples so far is its work
> with Gilead Sciences, a biopharmaceutical company that develops and
> commercializes medicine.
>
> The pharmaceutical company partnered with Spill to change how Black and Brown
> communities talk about sexual health and pre‑exposure prophylaxis (PrEP). The
> goal is to move the conversation beyond narrow associations and to include, for
> instance, cisgender Black women, who are often left out of HIV prevention
> messaging.

There's quite a few groups doing this type of research - to a smaller scale
since they're locally focused and aren't incentivized by profit to exist -
especially in the Black South - that would have benefited from this kind of
research. But instead of working _for_ Black people (which Spill never claims
to do, to be fair), they sell out the curated communities on there to pay back
what they owe to the investors who help them justify the platform's existence
(this is the myth [that Black capitalism sells us][26] - if Black people sell
Black people - or their essence thereof - back to the system we exist in,
_then_ we will see change).

## Threads for Attention, Spill for Money, Blacksky for ... What?

I write this as the maintainer of the Web experience of the revival of the
application that [popularized short form video][27]. It's niche in a way
&mdash; biased leading &mdash; in that folks are going to have to work _very
hard_ (i.e.: get creative) to try to sell you something. This is increased by
the fact that we don't have advertisement-driven feeds, like Threads or Spill,
nor do we have explicit chronological sorting of feeds (sans on profile pages),
like you'd see on Bluesky. It's also all open source so anyone could (attempt
to) fork the code and run their own setup. My _hope_ is that with the long term
vision of the open social Web, communities of color, nominally Black ones, can
_actually_ use technology in a way that redirects power _back_ to people.

So far, I think it's still early enough such that a perspective is to be seen as
non-tangible and only as idealistic. The notions of Black capitalism reinforce
the stances of metric-based community building (which incentivized folks from
working together) and instead focus on more negative-leading forms of community
building (by way of dunking and dragging). I think Blacksky provides an
opportunity for Black creators to _do something different_ and to actually live
to the values of autonomy that the creator economy likes to parade but without
having to commit to the NDA-driven culture it demands. This requires investment
&mdash; as does video editing, essay writing and any sort of work and does not
come cheap. But the payout, the investment will be generative enough to make
the effort spent seem insignificant.

I just hope that folks have and move with stances that are just as generative
and look for Black people online to build community with, not just to make
money off of them.
