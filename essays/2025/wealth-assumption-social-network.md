I've finished [Distributed Blackness][1] earlier today and I was left with more
questions than answers; though not too wide of a gap. I'll eventually
synthesize my thoughts into a review (of sorts) of the book. I do believe it's
the kind of material that those in the social Web sphere need to critically
digest as they continue to build "commons" meant to communicate with more and
more people &mdash; it's clear, even without reading that book, who and where
these places tend to be build for and at. As I read the book, it could
only go as far as the "observed" social Web, that is, the contents of the
collectable parts of social networks that researchers can freely access.
There's a bit of an irk I have to the idea of one's casual (or not) content on
the Web being made into data points &mdash; be it for research or profit.
However, thanks to the nature of surveillance baked into technoculture, this
isn't something we can easily avoid.

Switching to continue [Haiti: State Against Nation][2], a read I keep putting
up and down due to the glee of the unravelling of Haitian history, the need to
read the footnotes for more expansions and to grasp the historical contexts of
countries and policies at the time, has me thinking of present-day Haiti. The
division among the Haitian people across class hasn't changed since its
formation &mdash; for many reasons you'd find that class division amongst Black
people in the United States (and Canada and England) still exist: colorism,
generational wealth kept in conservative fashions (if possible) and good ol'
state sponsored disenfranchisement. Things like this contribute _greatly_ to
the kind of people who do end up being able to be online: although, as
mentioned in citations in _Distributed Blackness_, this has begun (but not have
been removed) to melt away due to the introduction of mobile computing. It's
ironic that a means of computing that requires more labor, resources and
capital allocation; higher than that of plantation labor, has managed to pull
folks from those same regions (if not similar) into the prized "digital public
square". Some twisted way to get the fruits of one's labor, I suppose.

> I have to stop and note that, somehow, if the [Civil Rights Act of 1964][4]
> didn't pass for another twenty years; or even ten, the digital landscape that
> we see today would have been dramatically different. I do believe that the
> (scantily enforced, due to the need for supplemental acts to give it teeth)
> protections the act provided is a part of the reason why we have video game
> cartridges and the Bash shell.

When I was on BlueSky, [Boris Mann][3] mentioned to me in a conversation chain
that included a mention of the global South and their computing capacity.
Having immediate family who lives in said place, the concept of connectivity in
those places aren't foreign to me. It's pretty incredible to see it develop and
immediately be demolished (indirectly by forces in the same countries that
they're developed in). This inability to capture these perspectives, these
voices &mdash; adding into the language barrier of those who aren't blessed
with the curiosity of accurate captions &mdash; creates a whole level of people who
are rendered invisible; even if their presences can be seen online, if ever. I
think about this a lot and the way that this also makes things like [digital
sharecropping][5] more apparent. This is _not_ a concern of protocol
developers, it seems, since the technology and compromises made _rely_ on
deifying the same barriers of access that we see today. Recent developments are
more about throwing a rope near the walled garden, instead of aiming to
bulldoze the damned thing down &mdash; reliance on a 430-person US-based NGO to
determine who and where can get a domain name - the _human-grade_ portal to the
Web, is one primary example. The cost of connectivity is _dramatically_ higher
for my family who rely on [a single company][dc] aiming to do what Google did
with the Internet with telecommunications in the Caribbean &amp; the owners
don't even _live_ in the countries that they service. Sounds like Dole's and
Nestle's origin story &mdash; but for the Internet. Doesn't help that the
investors in this company, outside of Denis, are United States _insurance_
companies.

I think a lot about how connectivity works, in theory, in places like Cuba; a
country who's so feared by the United States in developing an socioeconomic
order that China's made more concrete, that [the failed embargo system][6]
still impacts how folks can access the rest of the world, namely United
States-based services. Despite the United States delaying the deployment of a
undersea cable and fear of the Internet being good to Cuban economic development,
connectivity still grows, at a low 30% of the population. If Cuban Internet technology
was capable of growing _independent_ of the United States[^1] , how would have things like domain names
work? IP address allocation? How do you build something that works
independently of a central authority (like the Internet and most social
networks currently rely on)?

Answering that question is more useful than pushing three-generation old phones
in peoples' hands. It'd allow for local development versus the charity-colonial
angle we see developing today. I'm still wrestling through this idea with a
series of books but one thing remains consistently clear: the trajectory of the
fabled open social Web keeps repeating the same colonial undertones as the
conception of telecommunications itself from the 1700s. And even with the
growth of the libidinal economy online, we still observe only a _sliver_ of
what could be demonstrated online for a plethora of reasons who have a common
tendril: the need to see everyone, all the time.
