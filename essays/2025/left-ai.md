I posted a question briefly [on Bluesky][1] about the placement that leftists
can take when it comes to generative artificial intelligence. Since 2023, I've
passively explored the use cases of genAI, specifically in the case of software
development. My (recent) background has forced me to take a collectivist
perspective when looking at the impact of it on balancing labor's power.

## When Executives and Capitalists Drive Labor's Power

Despite what [someone who _probably_ makes more][2] than 70% of Americans
mentioned about the weakness of tech labor[^1], the scene has been [dramatically
swelling since 2020][7]. This has me looking at what opportunities management can,
will and have taken with new levers of control. This came to be of importance
after the sale of Twitter by Jack Dorsey and the board to Elon Musk, notably
when we saw how the company was able to keep running at moderately okay
[despite firing 6,000 people][4]. They dipped in valuation but capital
management, especially when in balance with the State helped bring it back up,
according to the [Wall Street Journal][5]. Granted, a lot of the workers who
_had_ the ability to find new work have ended up in places like Tiktok,
Facebook and Google &mdash; all places (sans Tiktok?) that also have
experienced a wave of layoffs. There's a need to keep in mind that as
generative AI becomes better[^2] at convincing management, there's more need to
put in levers _against_ hot swapping folks in favor of it. Although Elon didn't
have this in mind with his move, as far as we know; that kind of behavior
proved something that Thomas Ptacek of fly.io and Elon Musk agree on:

> LLMs really might displace many software developers. That’s not a high horse
> we get to ride. Our (software developers) jobs are just as much in tech’s
> line of fire as everybody else’s have been for the last 3 decades. We’re not
> East Coast dockworkers; we won’t stop progress on our own.

Despite being a statist (which Elon would also considered himself to be,
evidently by his eagerness to work as part of it briefly), they seem to be more
in favor of [private governance][6] versus public governance. I invite them to
reevaluate this position after reading this in full (if they ever do).Statists
are conventionally folks who are in favor of big goverance, and to ignore how
Musk _relied_ on strong (capitalist-friendly) goverance; the same way a _lot
of_ American tech companies do seems like an oversight by Thomas.

## Leveraging genAI for the Public?

The thing that folks do like to mention, especially in my left-leaning circles,
when it comes to generative AI, is China's introduction of smaller, cheaper and
efficient LLMs that can, at times, outperform the American made ones. This
seems to be a habit with Eastern technology &amp; from cars to computer
manufacturing. The most notable one is the ones produced by its namesake,
[DeepSeek][8]. As mentioned, I've been testing with these solutions at home,
most recently with [ollama][9], allowing me to flip between different
downloaded models[^3]. The output is okay &mdash; if I give it a "solved"
problem, it can get to a particular distance before I need to intervene and
correct things. But I struggle to replicate the level of performance that
[Harper produced with his journey into social "agentic" coding][11][^4]. The
focus here is what I see mirrored in public sector work: a want to "increase
response times" (or efficiency or whichever business-centric term you'd like to
improve) while not taking into consideration what _human_ decisions (almost
always policy) that cause slowdowns and the like[^5]. What's happening &mdash;
as it tends to and was even noted by Thomas Ptacek in his company's blog
&mdash; is that folks who champion these technologies _rarely_ stop to consider
how other people can use their tools for malice. The Wright brothers didn't
(couldn't?) and look how that turned out for the future of war and invasion.
The United States Marine Corps colonel, however, was already operating from a
position of violence, on behalf of the state and its interests, and had no
issue asking for even _more_ efficiency in how the M45 MEUSOC semi-auto pistol
can be used &mdash; especially in places like Iraq or by the Los Angeles Police
Department.

## Countermeasures in genAI

I opened up this mentioning a collectivist perspective. It's a novel one for
me, frankly, because I did see technology as a means of giving folks more power
in a world that has it held and hoarded by a few. It took me moving to
California, closer to the American crux of technology worship to come to terms
that it is largely an extension of the means in which capitalism operates. In
fact, [Ruha Benjamin][13]'s book, [Race As Technology][14] makes _many_ cases
&mdash; new to many, old to some &mdash; about how the most immediate
deployments of technology tend to have racial underpinnings to operate on
behalf of a larger agenda of integrating what she calls [The New Jim Code][18].
Before we can begin to talk about democratizing AI, making it fit some
definition of open; we have to be honest about who it's being made open
for and what we're defining as accessible. Routinely, this is not for the folks
who could benefit from having more control over their indirectly leased
technologies but for the folks who can afford thousand-dollar machines and
phones _off_-lease[^6]. To this day, technology is made and optimized in the
perspective of a white man. We have small efforts towards changing
this but they're not just far and few &mdash; they're intentionally underfunded
and driven out of sight. Relying on the mimicry of capital to drive a new
direction will result in its trend of burnout or worse for the founders and
workers involved.

### Requirements for Legitimacy in AI-Driven/Native/First Tools

What's needed is more research &mdash; ideally publicly funded &mdash; that
pushes folks to integrate a means of safety into these tools. It'd be a mix of
things that we've seen folks &mdash; mainly folks routinely targeted by those
who historically weaponize technology against others &mdash; ask for in these
tools:

- **A legitimate right of exit**: this means being able to remove oneself in
  information included in datasets used for large language models and working on
  adjusting how we train models. Instead of the vacuuming approach; we can lean
  into targeted sets that rely on licensed (publicly or permissive) media and
  material that reduce the need to constantly retrain for deletion. It's not as
  useful, in experiments of mine, to try to retrain a larger model to remove
  information &mdash; especially since we don't really tag _each_ parameter to
  what bit of information it came from. Math made things ambigious and at the
  same, not at all.

- **Aggressive scoping of tools**: I recently built a MCP server for work that
  was designed to be extremely restrictive on what information it could obtain
  (to a point where it falls back to generating text of a canned list of things
  you can prompt it on) and I found that to be a "happy" place for AI apologists
  and admonishers alike: it being _very_ clear on its purpose and what it can
  access. Another approach to this is by using [RAG][15] to scope down the output
  of these tools. However, we cannot expect companies like Microsoft or Google to
  do this when their objectives are to be "global" information producers via
  ChatGPT or Gemini.

- **Opt-out levers**: Folks should _not have to_ be submitted to systems
  that'll constantly train (and train) using their information. Facebook and
  Google have made this into a business practice with our social lives and
  curiousity respectively but doing this as a mode of business has to be
  curtailed the same way we like to speak about gun control. Unfortunately,
  because these companies provide immensive value to the state and control a
  trememdous amount of business and tech influence; we'll continue to see
  companies like Notion, Slack and the like aim to reproduce the "productivity"
  and "usefulness" gains of these technologies not in hopes of staying relevant
  but of being the "Google" of their lane.

I don't think a lot of the above will happen. I don't necessarily aim for [an
easy victory][16] for the people either &mdash; easy victories are easily
undone. But what does work is pushing as hard as possible across a spectrum
because the fight is a protracted one that's only come to the immediate
forefront because it's escaped the containment of class. In [Black
Software][17], there's mention of police departments and government agencies
that leaned on the same company that provided compute for Nazis to tend turn
towards "improving" policing in Black communities. These projects evovled into
the basis of artificial intelligence technologies used by the New York Police
Department, a law enforcement department with enough funding that can rival
universities. I make this parallel because, as mentioned before, technologists
and business-minded folks routinely have opportunities to do something
productive for society and communities. However, they use the excuse of the
market and investors to turn _away_ from the such, like Jack Dorsey admitted to
on a podcast, and opt to "let the market deal with it".
