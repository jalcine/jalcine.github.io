As I continue to look for work in this [poisoned
industry](/essays/2026/kicked-out.md), I've tried to pay attention to what
folks in the space are saying about it and determine if I really need to
consider stepping out. Laurie's interview highlights quite a few things that I
found poignant:

- "only white guys can afford not to go to college": I never went to college
  because my family didn't understand it (non-English, uneducated immigrants)
  and because of how my high school life went, I was immediately written off.
  Software engineering as a trade _saved_ me [in some
  capacity](/essays/2024/regrets-with-tech.md).
- "the thing we found in senior engineering has become central to the job":
  this _means_ that there's no longer any space for on-the-job training of the
  skills that would have normally skilled people up; there's _negative_
  incentive to have folks become articulate into these roles
- "we don't know who the big players are": the _AI vendors_. They literally are
  the biggest players; operating like Stripe when it comes to understanding which
  smaller businesses are rising or falling since we're submitting _all_ of our
  information (the new data/product) over to them.

There's a very different perspective that folks who are _invested_ in this
industry's bubble _not happening within the next five years_ versus those who
might be a bit more like me, who doesn't completely mind the death of the role
(at least in its capital hoarding way) but is concerned about the lever that's
been released in favor of letting statistical models drive business decisions.

It is a _lot easier_ to be optimistic about generative AI when you're:

- comfortably employed
- have sonority that allows them to move through interviews faster
- have already been in the industry for at least twenty years (or ten)

This makes them ignorant to the day-to-day[^1] that they _never_ have to engage
in; making these optimistic stances reductive. What's more likely to happen is
_what's_ happening now: increased ramps on layoff cycles as technologists are
submitting their knowledge to machines that'll produce a lot more _closed
source_ technologies (there's no point of contributing to the commons - there's
less of a competitive advantage if you can throw Jev, Muse and Gemini at the
monster of a codebase). This is even _mentioned_ in the podcast - the rise of
"(generated) personalized software".

I suggest you read more of Laurie's stances so you can see the gap (or rather
the executive perspective) in how this is and how this works in real life (no
matter what Markdown files scanning spreadsheets show you):

- <https://seldo.com/posts/do-ai-enabled-companies-need-fewer-people/>
- <https://seldo.com/posts/ai-has-torched-the-market-for-junior-programmers/>
- <https://seldo.com/posts/we-are-all-product-engineers-now/>

Then come up, watch that video and tell me how much money, thought and concern
is _actually_ being put into junior software development (none), employment
stability (none) and the durability of the career path that is software
engineering (very little). Feeding into the hype is no different that investing
in the wet labs!
