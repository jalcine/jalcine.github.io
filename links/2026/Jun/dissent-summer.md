Patrick Iber opened up this issue in an almost prophetic way with [the editor's opening][1]:

> The semiquincentennial celebrations planned by the White House will not be a
> demonstration of unity. They will celebrate the domination of one part of the
> country over the other. Donald Trump’s 250th will be a carnival, complete
> with a barker-in-chief, attempting to sell the idea that the United States is
> winning like never before. Outside of MAGA-land, there will not be so much
> celebrating. It is hard to cheer when Trump will insist that the festivities
> are made into a reflection of his glory.

This section was _immediately_ highlighted because it couldn't have been
phrased any better. I do wish that the lawsuit to [stop the event from
happening][2] landed but relying on the legal system to enforce any sort of
justice is like asking CitiBank nicely to [return the money they stole from the
Haitian National Bank][3] &mdash; a joke all around. I disagree with quite a
bit of what Iber continues on to say because it clings to this idea that we can
just refactor the "bad parts" of the American project and lean on "small-d
democratic control, not big-D Democratic control" to fix the ailments of this
project. The first essay after the note operates like a palette cleanser
because it's _way more honest_ and direct about the place that the United
States has in its evolution as a global power that's attempting to drag the
rest of the world down with it.

## [The American Revolution in Global Retreat][4]

![An illustration of the American flag by Anna Sorokina](https://dissentmagazine.org/wp-content/files_mf/1779979629AzizThumb.jpg)
This essay prompted a few posts I made on Bluesky as I was reading it on my
flight to ATL Monday evening[^1]. I found it a great way to reframe the way
other countries try to get the favor of the United States in their own
political ventures - like India and Indonesia:

> In his 1949 speech to the U.S. Congress two years after Indian independence,
> Prime Minister Jawaharlal Nehru called the American founders the
> “torchbearers of freedom, not only for this country but for the world.”
> Indonesian President Sukarno opened the famed 1955 Bandung Conference of
> Asian and African states by highlighting the anniversary of Paul Revere’s
> midnight ride, which informed settler militias of advancing British forces
> prior to the battles of Lexington and Concord. Sukarno declared the Third
> World’s anticolonial struggle to be the culmination of a “battle which began
> 180 years ago” with “the American War of Independence, the first successful
> anti-colonial war in history.”
>
> During the twentieth century, U.S. independence became a symbolic touchstone
> for many communities in the Global South that would have been excluded from
> that revolt. It stood for the idea that all people have the right to govern
> themselves and to fundamentally change the conditions of their own societies.
> For all the flaws of the American Century, its great attraction was the
> degree to which economic, political, and cultural life seemed to exist in a
> dynamic relationship with the will of its changing people. Above all, it was
> this relationship that gave world-historical significance to the events of
> 1776 for outsiders. Rather than a local political conflict unleashed by
> restive settlers, the long-term engine of American renewal meant that an
> eighteenth-century spark could be linked to an unfolding global story of
> human freedom.

The focus it brought to the global conversation: that the American Revolution
was _the grandest_ and most noteworthy is refuted by C.L.R James, as mentioned
in this piece. If the mission of independence, of freedom - as described by
Thomas Jefferson and F.W. Reitz - is tied to one's _authority_ to "exterminate
the brutes" and invoke "manifest day", it makes sense why then the ceiling of
political understanding that Black capitalist and celebrities have are that of
house Negro-esque ideas: staying as close to whiteness and mimicing its talking
points (despite the cementing in history of cultural production it might have
brought - the cost is intellectual and communal damage for the profit of a
few):

> The State Department may have sponsored jazz tours, but in the era of
> Coltrane such art often remained inflected with global anticolonial
> solidarity in ways that challenged any easy Cold War purpose. These
> oppositional connections certainly did not disappear, but by the
> twenty-first-century age of Black billionaires like Jay-Z and Oprah,
> commercial creativity seemed to fit far more comfortably with both American
> capital and power.
