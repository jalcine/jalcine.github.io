Well, well, well - this is a bit of a doozy. Not shocking or surprising, but a doozy nonetheless.
[This report from Stanford][1] highlights quite a few things:

- AI hiring tools are helping folks break the (weakly enforced) law around hiring bias.
- Blackbox (proprietary) systems help shield companies from this bias-as-a-service.
- "Black applicants are the most likely to be adversely impacted: **30% of Black applicants apply to at least one position that demonstrates adverse impact against Black applicants**." For tech companies, this number is definitely _way higher_.

I wrote about my frustration, despite having a tenured background, in [reading
a book on "tech resumes"][2] and [on the job hunt itself][3]. I don't _believe_
folks are entitled to a job - I do think that if we're not going to provide
durable and reliable employment, that we need to have society provide a social
net with _all of this excess capital_ such that we don't end up criminalizing
them for things that jobs provide like the means of paying for rent, buying
food and at this point not being sick. That's the silent part of the idea of
labor: we're not entitled to security but we are _guaranteed_ social exile (by
homelessness) at best and prison at worst when forced to make moves undesirable
to the "job makers."

A bit extreme from the realm of tech workers - especially the highly paid ones
like myself (perhaps when you're not floating two families) - but it's the
truth of the system that we silently enable by not doing anything about it. I
do _not_ expect people whose job is largely to speak on the greatness of
industry ([Lenin was right][4] about <https://www.economist.com/>, as a subscriber
for two years now) to seriously address this. That's a problem because they
become the _canonical_ voice of industry and there's a class of peers - kinfolk
or not - who use them as a barometer of how good the industry is going despite
reported issues being held up elsewhere.
