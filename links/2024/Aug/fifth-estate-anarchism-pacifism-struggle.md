Pacifism as a concept perplexes me[^1] so this reading of the role that these two concepts,
anarchism and pacifism, had in contributing to the anti-war movement was interesting. However, this
review has made some statements that I can't disagree with. It opens up strong:

> Violence is not all the same. Context matters. There’s something much worse about violence
> when it’s perpetrated by or with the tacit acceptance of the State. It’s not just that governments
> and their allies in the capitalist class and the patriarchy have more resources, more weapons, and
> fewer ethical qualms about killing than most. Beyond these obvious assets, they can hide behind
> the veil of legitimacy that the State (allegedly) offers them. Hitler, George W. Bush, and Derek
> Chauvin may not have a lot in common personally, but all committed their crimes under the reasonable
> presumption that the social and political order sanctioned such behavior.

This statement is bold and can cause a knee-jerk reaction depending on who you are. If you're
accepting of American violence, putting George W. Bush wouldn't make sense until you consider that
his war led to _three million_ innocent Iraqis dying over a lie on weapons of mass destruction.
Chauvin had a twisted agenda[^2] but would be considered a villian to most Americans, especially
because of his figure, the mask falling.

This book is interesting but not compelling enough for me to engage. I respect pacifism as a concept
but I have yet to see a concrete example of it working outside of the West[^3]. How do you suppose
the [Sahel belt][1] practice pacifism, for example? This is a reminder, for me and readers, that
solutions aren't (can't!) be universal. But that doesn't mean that they shouldn't be taken (if
viable). The review continues, however, with an indication of post-World War II America:

> Repeatedly in Akst’s book and the contemporary writings of people like Muste and Dellinger, pacifists
> made the inconvenient point that in the name of winning an all-out war against Hitler, the U.S. was
> adopting many aspects of Hitlerism itself.

The astute reader who expands the perspective to be beyond the West will note that _it is_ valid.
This was also a time when the US government and its allies also were manipulating so many African and
Asiatic countries in attempts to maintain their control over the land. The reviewer seems to have a
particular ignorance towards anything that non-white folks (sans Martin Luther King Jr and Bayard
Rustin) were doing. They write, at the time, "the new radicals of the 1960s never [finding] a
way to engage the American labor movement in their struggle, or generate an alternative for
working-class people in their increasingly complacent and ineffective mainstream union
establishment". Questions like "who were able to make up union representation at this time" come to
mind. Bayard Rustin was also a very prominent figure for labor as a high ranking representative for
AFL-CIO; who, around this time, gave way for police to be more concretely recognized as workers. No
one is perfect.
