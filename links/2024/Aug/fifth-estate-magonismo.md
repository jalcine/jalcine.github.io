This book review _immediately_ introduced me to quite a bit that I had to look up. Before we had the FBI
as it stood today, we had the National Bureau of Criminal Identification in 1896. It was seemingly formed
in response (if not to help track) the rise of anarchism in the United States, specifically after
the [assassination of President McKinley][1]. I didn't know a president outside of JFK was killed and
for it to be specifically an anarchist is huge, given the way America's strength against dissent
is heavily curtailed today. All of this seemed to be at the _strong_ urges of President Theodore
Roosevelt.[^1]

What freaked me out is how this was effectively _instrumental_ in the formation of the modern day
police force. As mentioned in [Prisons By Any Other Name][2], there's a history of Texan rangers
attacking Indigenous people (who are the modern day ancestors of Mexicans). I quoted a portion about
this on [Bookwyrm][3], but below is what came to mind:

> Volunteer police helpers also have a long history in the United States of facilitating Indigenous
> genocide. In the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, for example, the Texas Rangers chased
> down and killed Native people rumored to have attacked colonizers, and they lynched Tejanos, or
> Texans of Mexican descent. But the Texas Rangers were not actual police, military or even militia
> forces; instead, these volunteer vigilantes were essentially "community members" playing a
> collaborating role in policing and protecting newly arriving white colonizers.

It's truly horrifying to understand that the symbolism of that badge comes from a history of
genocide on "American" soil. This review continues with the journey and assaults that the Magon
brothers experienced during their efforts to liberate Mexico. Some motivations that pushed people
were things like [labor repression in copper mines in 1906][4] as well as [the forced migration of
Indigenous people to Yucatan][5] - all around 1906. This ties in with the _Brown Belt_ of Jim Crow
like law ([Juan Crow][6], as it's called) being pushed on Mexican Americans.

There's so much more that this article goes into. I _really_ want to read this book! If there's
one thing I don't know much about, it's definitely the rise and formation of Mexico and its
development as a country (as well as those who fight for liberation within it).
