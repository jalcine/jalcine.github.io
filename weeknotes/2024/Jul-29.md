## [The Socialist Challenge Today][6]
I've (regretfully) started this book, mainly due to its short size. The book aims to capture the difficulty of the
formation of a socialist (or socialist-orientated) nation-state in the Western world. The calamity of [the SYRIZA party
in Greece][10] was hard to read, due to the seemingly infinite roadblocks that they'd endure and the nature of being
trapped within the Eurozone. Any action that they took would have to contest with global capitalism - a creation of the
European nations that plagues them to this day. I ended up finishing it today and found the closing notes a bit sober
(if not pointed):

> The profound political dilemma this book has tried to confront is this: for a socialist-led government in the current
> conjuncture, giant steps toward transformative change would be impossible, but small steps risk being swallowed into
> the logic of the system. Even meeting the first condition for building on electoral success&mdash;i.e., immediately
> delivering material gains for the working class&mdash;entails being honest about the obstacles in the way. Taxing the
> 1 percent&mdash;or the top 5 or 10 percent&mdash;is a good idea for all kinds of obvious reasons. The potential gains
> from under taxed and untaxed corporate and financial income and assets, not to mention personal wealth taxes,
> inheritance taxes, luxury taxes, and so forth are enormous. But the capacity to do this without effective capital
> controls needs to be faced squarely. Moreover, the idea that taxing the "billionaire class" would be sufficient to pay
> for the impressive range of policies proposed by socialists today is clearly fanciful&mdash;and tends to
> underestimate the need for weaning the working class itself away from the anti-tax propaganda of the neo-liberal era.

It's meant to be a _honest_ perspective but I do think there's a bit of an immediate concession on what _could_ work and
a hyper-focus on federal level reforms that, if coupled with state changes, like the one designed by Bernie in Vermont,
could make this transition a lot easier. However, it doesn't seem like the authors have much faith (or excluded
consideration) of state-level pressure that could be applied to push for federal change in a meaningful way. It's as if
by shooting for the highest target, one is destined to fail. They do follow up with something that's not really
unchallengeable without engaging in some form of nation-state violence:

> An overriding limit in all of the steps toward taking control over economic life, even in the case of relatively
> modest expansions of social provision, is the power of capital to exit and invest abroad (along with the refusal of
> capital to keep coming in).

We see the above most nominally in places like Cuba, where the United States and its allies use the weight of
nation-state commerce levers like embargoes and sanctions to restrict the ability of a country to build independence of
its own means. This practice is a readily available aspect of Western capital via the levers of nation-states and it's
an old practice. In another book, [Haiti: State Against Nation][17], Trouillot highlights how European countries used this
as a way to weaken Haiti's fledging economy out the gate:

> At the first testing ground of neocolonialism, Haiti experienced the somber implications of that policy for the third
> world very early. As a neocolony of two industrializing countries, it collected from neither the now-expected (even if
> meager and unsatisfactory) returns of the post-colonial order. Foreign aid was not fashionable&mdash;not even in the
> name of strategic interests; nor were development programs. The United Nations did not exist; nor did the World Bank,
> the IMF, the Organization of American States, or the British Commonwealth. Haiti's trajectory was against the current;
> its very existence defied the geopolitical odds. It was an agricultural exporter, yet imported primarily agricultural
> products&mdash;indeed a peasant country where food topped the list of imports. It was a country born of a
> revolutionary war, yet one subordinated to the military power of Europe and the United States. ... At a time when
> European nations were extending their sway in African and when U.S forces were occupying the Philippines, Cuba and
> Puerto Rico, Haiti stood alone with Liberia and Ethiopia, black states in a modern international order where political
> independence was associated&mdash;as if in nature&mdash;with whiteness. It was a country without national allies, an
> international anachronism.

I will say, for a book that wants a socialist world, The Socialist Challenge focused a lot on the West (understandably)
but failed to connect aspects of national and foreign policy that reinforces the control and mobility of capital that we
see today[^2].

## [How Europe Underdeveloped Africa][7]
Rodney makes some points that remind me _a lot_ of interactions I've seen today. Namely, emphasis made by me:

> To ease their guilty consciences, Europeans try to throw the major responsibility for the slave trade on to the
> Africans. One European author of a book on the slave trade (appropriately entitled [Sins of Our Father][8]) explained
> how many other white people urged him to state that the trade was the responsibility of African chiefs, and that
> Europeans merely turned up to buy the captives &mdash; _as though without European demand there would have been captives
> sitting on the beach by the millions_.

I'm looking for a copy of that book to read because I'm curious about how these narratives were crafted. It was
published in the 1970s in England so I'm expecting a _smidgen_ of white victimhood being made into the central point of
the book. It's safe to say that this book, and I'm only about a quarter in, is filling in the gaps I have in my
understanding of the relationship that Europe had with Africa. Another point that stood out to me was Rodney's expansion
on how "Europe became the center of a world-wide system and that it was European capitalism which set slavery and the
Atlantic slave trade it motion". He writes:

> Furthermore, when one European nation challenged another to obtain captives from an African ruler, Europe benfitted
> from whichever of the two nations won the conflict. Any European trader could arrive on the coast of West Africa and
> exploit the political differences which he found there. For example, in the small territory that the Portuguese later
> claimed as Guinea-Biassau, there were more than a dozen ethnic groups. It was so easy to see one off against another
> that the Europeans called it a "slave trader's paradise".

This invoked the imagery of the current "competition" that the United States and Canada had in picking which eager 
member of AFRICOM's watchful eye would be selected to send their troops to Haiti in order to "control the gang violence"
that's gone to the national stage. In this case, Kenya, at the whim of William Rufo, is working with the PNH (Haiti's
national police), decided to [pick up that mantle last year][9]. Embarrassing. Perhaps more embarrassing as wearing white
[wigs in an African state house][11]. Decolonization is a lifestyle and journey.

I wrote two posts last week. One was about my [growing frustration in finding a job][2]. I've gotten some great chances
to talk to folks on how to improve my chances and folks have been trying to connect me with roles. However, it's
becoming more clear that positioning seems to be less of a reliable option and that a direct referral might be the only

The other post was about the end of [my discussion of electoral politics online][3]. I gave reasons that reinforce my
want to distance myself from the conversation. Outside of being directly blamed online for the preemptive election of a
Republican candidate[^1], I'm choosing to actively disengage with people who try to "educate" me on things. I
_routinely_ talk about my perspective on American, African and politics of the global South, being a party member of the
[All African People's Revolutionary Party][5] and a labor organizer; it's _necessary_ to understand as much as possible
of the _global_ context in which the world operates for one's activism to be meaningful beyond a little kickback.
