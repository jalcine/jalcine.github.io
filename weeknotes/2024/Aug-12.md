# Reading

## Prison By Any Other Name
[This book][2] was written by two abolitionists, one notably the EIC of Truthout. I'm finding this read to be more
guiding and tangible for me than [Prisons Make Us Safer][1]. This is because it moves a bit from the
introduction of aspects around the need for abolition and focuses on how existing companies, politicians
and other institutions have a _vested stake_ in maintaining these systems. It's also highlighting
the lives of people who are forced to electronic monitoring (or EM) and the aggressive restricting
that end up _encouraging_ recidivism (the chances of re-entering jail or prison). It's hard not to
see and get curious about the relationships of these companies mentioned to the industry I'm wading
through.

## Prisons Make Us Safer
I finished [this book][1] last week. It was written a year after [Prisons By Any Other Name][2], both by
the same author. I commend their ability to knock these stories out; they're not light and do a very
difficult job of making plain institutional and commercial violence very plain. I want to write
about this book because Law's done a great job of setting up common refrains for keeping prison
infrastructure intact, like how it helps to keep children safe (it doesn't), how most people use
abuse as an excuse to avoid jail (they don't), how prison is only an issue for men (it's _really
not_), or how it's the most reliable way to keep us safe from rapists and murders (it's not). Each
of these chapters bring you to talk about a point as to _how_ these points go so popular and walks
you through the lives of people impacted by these myths. It all
returns to highlight one underlying aspect of society: that **we are way more comfortable breaking
up community than to work on healing or developing it**.

## You Exist Too Much
[This book][3] is one I'm returning to[^1]. Arafat brings us into the mind of a young adult struggling
with a particular addiction and identity crisis of both romantic identity and that of being a
Palestinian-American. There's moments where I learn quite a bit about what it means to hold this
kind of dual identity from someone of a different (and more recently publicized) background. There's
also the growth (or steps towards &mdash; I'm not done yet!) of the main character aiming to grow a
sense of self outside of what ails them &mdash; understanding what does it _really_ mean to be
loved. Excited to see how this book ends but she needs to tighten up!

## No More Heroes
I opened [this book][4] on a whim because something about deconstructing the role of saviorship in
mainstream media came to mind and this book had the word mentioned in it when I was searching my
to-be-read list. The foreword by Roxanne Ortiz _blew me away_:

> The author correctly begins this book with the Christian Crusades in 1096, and jumping across the
> Atlantic in 1492, as the foundation for the founding of a Republic (the United States) to “rescue”
> the Native. The very origin story of the United States forms the core of the savior project. In
> November 1620, English religious dissidents (“Pilgrims”) landed on the shore of what is now Cape
> Cod, Massachusetts. Forty-one of the “Pilgrims,” all men, wrote and signed the Mayflower Compact,
> named after the ship they arrived on. Invoking God’s name and declaring themselves loyal subjects
> of the king, the signatories announced that they had journeyed to North America “to plant the
> First Colony” and did therefore “Covenant and Combine ourselves together in a Civil Body Politic”
> to be governed by “just and equal Laws” enacted “for the general good of the Colony, unto which we
> promise all due submission and obedience.” A decade later, when additional English settlers
> founded the Massachusetts Bay Colony, they adopted an official seal designed in England prior to
> their journey. The central image depicts a near-naked native holding a harmless, flimsy-looking
> bow and arrow and inscribed with the plea, “Come over and help us.” Nearly three hundred years
> later, the official seal of the U.S. military veterans of the “Spanish-American War” (the invasion
> and occupation of Puerto Rico, Cuba, and the Philippines) showed a dark, naked woman kneeling
> before an armed U.S. soldier and a sailor, with a U.S. battleship in the background. One may trace
> this recurrent altruistic theme into the early twenty-first century, when the United States still
> invades countries under the guise of rescue.

I'm _definitely_ going to keep reading this after Black August.

Eventually, this will be a _bit more_ dynamic of a space; something that has links that I find worth
writing about on my site and linking those notes here. It's a bit annoying to pull it out of
Obsidian from here. Yet another thing to work on!

* [Inquest: Ferguson At Ten][web_7]: This one was a hard read. Inquest did a great job of finding
  folks to help recap these moments. I think what's forgotten is the _kind_ of administration we
  lived under, how much vitrol folks saw from the bipartianship committees and how almost _nothing_
  has changed to prevent these events from happening again.
* [Salon: Noah Lyles' collapse underscores our collective COVID denial][web_1]: I'm upset for Lyles and the
  fact that we, as a society, have invested more resources in pretending that COVID hasn't happened
  _at all_. We have learned very little socially from the impacts of past pandemics and are more
  focused on the _performance_ of a functional society as opposed to working to keep the health of
  it up. The fact that in _four years_, [1.2 million Americans have died from it][web_1.1] shows this
  indifference quite loudly &mdash; and that's what been reported _as_ deaths from COVID. We don't
  need to [rely on the state to take care of us][web_1.2], however. It's a lot easier when our tax
  dollars can go to care and not committing genocide.
* [Comradery: Project Pages Update][web_2]: Their first update in over a year(!), the cooperatively
  owned patronage platform is getting close to a beta release! I'm always eager to see folks explore
  this space.
* [Truthout: Last Week, 9 in 10 Americans Had No Opinion of Walz. That's About to Change][web_3]: 1
  out of 10 is still around 30 million people in the United States. That aside, Truthout provided a
  pretty balanced report on Walz and a set of stances as to why folks should be interested in
  throwing their support behind him. Despite my stances on artificial harm reduction; I think that
  having folks we can push is important. [Labor seems to agree][web_3.1], although some unions are
  being more pointed [about the hypocrisy held by the state][web_3.2].
* [Clear Thinking: Can astrologers use astrological charts to understand people's character and
  lives? Our new study put astrologers to the test][web_4]: Astrology is something I have ... no
  real opinion on, to be honest. I try to understand it and have been combing over a book that
  [Ra'il][rail] has suggested. I have friends who are practitioners and frankly, I have a _bit more
  confidence_ in this, mainly because it requires to _actually_ understand the person in question.
  However, this study _does not help_ astrology as a concept (and flattens it - there's _many
  different kinds_ of it) and shows some of the weakness of using empirical (secular) systems to
  define things that escape its measurements.
* [Ra'il: Black August 2024][web_5]: Speaking of Ra'il, here's their plan on observing Black August!
  They have a different approach than I, which I appreicate because it shows the _need_ to break
  from any sort of uniformity on how this goes down. The tenants are clear; the people diverse and
  so the practices have to reflect that. If you haven't yet, I recommend [subscribing to their
  Patreon][rail] to support their work and [their newsletter][rail:newsletter].
* [Prism Reports: Kamala Harris and the Erasure of Palestine][web_6]: Something progressives and
  Democrats will repeat is the need for a "two-state solution", like the ones deployed in the United
  States and Indigineious nations that they've occupied. Harris is no different. Her background with
  Zionism is something I'm not too surprised about, in order to be a politican, you have to toe the
  line &mdash; no breaking from the talking points. One of those, for both Democrats and
  Republicans, is to see the complete eradication of Palestine from the map by way of the $800
  billion+ US-sponsored genocide. The idea of "assimilation" is one that hits personally as the need
  for any non-Western European Anglo-presenting people to have _everyone else_ adopt their
  identities, cultures, languages and lifestyles is something that still plagues us today. Now in
  the places where I live, I wouldn't be shocked if Harris does nothing to [stop the increasing
  censure of free speech][web_6.1].
* [Prison Journalism Project: On Burger Day in Prison, I Went Back for Seconds][web_8]: This hit
  hard. As part of Black August, I'm fasting, a practice meant to demonstrate solidarity with
  prisoners due to the inhumane approach to care that they receive. But to hear that a burger,
  something I can _order on my phone_ if I'm lazy or make myself if I go outside to get ingredients,
  is made into something of a "reward". To learn that the act of bartering is illegal in Florida
  prisons, a practice that economists _love to tout_ as the crux of the evolution of their field
  also frustates me but doesn't surprise me. Prisons are designed to dehumanize. The author, who's
  incarcerated in Everglades Correctional Instution out here in Florida, writes [a lot about
  food][web_8.1].
